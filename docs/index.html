Executive Summary

TSMC is the world's largest dedicated semiconductor foundry, manufacturing chips designed by other companies rather than selling its own. It holds roughly 67.6% of the global foundry market (Q1 2026) and produces an estimated ~90% of the world's most advanced logic chips (3-nanometer and below) — a concentration of capability with no real substitute anywhere else on earth.

The investment case has been transformed over the past two years by the AI buildout. High-performance computing (HPC) now accounts for 66% of quarterly revenue, up from a smartphone-led mix historically, and Nvidia has overtaken Apple as TSMC's single largest customer in 2026 — a structural shift, not a one-quarter blip. Full-year 2025 revenue reached $121.8 billion (+37% YoY) with net income of $54.1 billion (a ~44% net margin); Q2 2026 revenue was $40.2 billion (+36% YoY) at a 67.7% gross margin, among the highest in TSMC's history.

The company is responding to both AI demand and geopolitical pressure by dramatically expanding outside Taiwan: its committed Arizona investment has snowballed from roughly $65 billion to $265 billion as of July 2026, alongside 2026 capex guidance raised to $60–64 billion. This sits against the single largest risk to the thesis: TSMC's manufacturing base remains overwhelmingly concentrated in Taiwan, a jurisdiction under persistent geopolitical pressure from China, and no amount of financial analysis fully hedges that tail risk.

Company Overview

TSMC was founded on February 21, 1987 by Morris Chang, a former Texas Instruments executive, after he convinced the Taiwanese government to back a dedicated chip manufacturer that would compete with none of its customers by never designing or selling its own chips — the pure-play foundry model. It remains headquartered in Hsinchu, Taiwan.

Chang served as CEO from 1987 to 2005 and as Chairman until 2018. Mark Liu and C.C. Wei took over as co-CEOs in 2013; C.C. Wei has been sole CEO since 2018 and added the Chairman title in June 2024. Under his tenure, annual revenue has grown roughly 3.6x, from about $34 billion (2018) to $122 billion (2025). The company employs approximately 77,000 people and trades in the US as an ADR (NYSE: TSM), with its primary listing on the Taiwan Stock Exchange (2330.TW).

Why the foundry model matters
Because TSMC never competes with its own customers, fabless giants that are fierce rivals to each other — Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm — are all comfortable using the same manufacturer. That neutrality, compounded over decades into deep process know-how, is arguably TSMC's most durable structural advantage.

Business Model & Structural Analysis

Manufacturing footprint

TSMC's most advanced production remains concentrated in Taiwan across its Hsinchu, Tainan, and Taichung "Gigafab" sites. It is actively diversifying, though from a small base: a joint-venture fab in Kumamoto, Japan (with Sony and Denso), a fab in Dresden, Germany (the ESMC joint venture with Bosch, Infineon, and NXP), and a rapidly escalating commitment in Arizona, US. Independent reporting places TSMC's most advanced production lines — including 2nm and below — as staying in Taiwan through at least the end of the decade, even as the Arizona footprint grows.

Technology leadership

TSMC's 2-nanometer (N2) process has crossed a 60%+ yield rate — the threshold generally considered stable volume production — using gate-all-around (GAA) transistor architecture for the first time, offering an estimated 10–15% better performance, 25–30% lower power draw, and 15% higher transistor density versus 3nm. Customer concentration has followed the same shift. Nvidia is estimated to represent roughly 22% of 2026 revenue (~$33B), edging out Apple at roughly 18% (~$27B) — the first time in TSMC's history that Apple has not been its largest customer. The concentration is even sharper in advanced packaging: Nvidia is estimated to hold roughly 60% of CoWoS capacity, with Nvidia, Broadcom, and AMD together accounting for an estimated 85%+ of it. CoWoS has effectively become as important a bottleneck — and as important a moat — as the leading-edge node itself, and it is reportedly sold out well into 2027.

Margins have expanded materially — from ~39.5% net margin in FY2023 to gross margins now running in the mid-to-high 60s — a direct result of pricing power on leading-edge nodes and a richer, AI-driven product mix.

Capital intensity & balance sheet

TSMC raised its 2026 capex guidance from an initial $52–56 billion range to $60–64 billion, with 70–80% earmarked for advanced-node capacity — among the largest capital programs of any company in the world, reflecting management's confidence that AI-driven demand persists (CEO commentary has pointed to robust demand "through 2030"). The balance sheet remains a clear net-cash position, giving the company latitude to fund this buildout largely without leverage.

Industry & Competitive Landscape

The leading-edge foundry market is, in practice, a three-horse race — and arguably closer to a one-horse race with two credible challengers still catching up.

The structural barriers keeping this field so narrow are extreme: tens of billions of dollars in annual capex, decades of accumulated process know-how, multi-year customer qualification cycles, and a near-monopoly held by ASML (Netherlands) on the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography equipment required for leading-edge nodes. None of that is easily replicated, which is exactly why TSMC's share has proven so durable.

Geopolitical & Political Risk

Taiwan produces an estimated ~60% of the world's semiconductors and ~90% of its most advanced chips. This concentration underpins the "silicon shield" theory: that Taiwan's manufacturing importance is itself a deterrent against Chinese military action, since a disruption would be catastrophic enough to the global economy that outside powers — principally the US — would be compelled to intervene. Whether that deterrent effect is strengthening or eroding as China accelerates its own chip self-sufficiency push and as TSMC diversifies production outside Taiwan is genuinely contested among analysts, and it is the single largest, least-hedgeable risk in this entire investment case.

2026 policy timeline
  • Aug 2022 — CHIPS Act: ~$52.7B in US subsidies plus a 25% investment tax credit for domestic semiconductor manufacturing; the original policy driver behind TSMC's Arizona commitment.
  • Dec 31, 2025: the Validated End-User (VEU) exemption expired for TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix's China operations — each must now apply annually for US export licenses.
  • Jan 14, 2026 — Section 232 tariffs: a new 25% tariff on certain advanced semiconductor imports, intended to push manufacturing onshore.
  • Jan 15, 2026 — US-Taiwan trade deal: Taiwan committed $250B of investment into US manufacturing in exchange for reduced tariffs on Taiwanese chips and equipment; the US Commerce Secretary has stated a goal of bringing ~40% of Taiwan's chip supply chain to the US.
  • Jan 15, 2026 — BIS export rule change: license review for advanced chips (e.g. Nvidia H200, AMD MI325X) bound for China/Macau shifted from "presumption of denial" to case-by-case review, conditional on US-based third-party testing to verify the chips don't exceed permitted specifications.
  • Jul 2026: TSMC lifted its total committed Arizona investment to $265B, adding at least four more fabs (2nm and below) plus advanced packaging capacity.

Read together, US policy is a double-edged sword for the thesis. The CHIPS-era incentives and the 2026 Taiwan trade deal materially reduce geopolitical tail risk by deepening the US-Taiwan economic relationship and are being met with real capital commitments rather than resistance. But the export-control regime constrains the addressable market for the AI chips TSMC's customers sell into China — this hits TSMC indirectly, through reduced demand from Nvidia/AMD for China-bound parts, rather than through TSMC's own customer relationships, since TSMC does not itself export finished AI accelerators to China. Onshoring also carries a structural cost: US-based fabs are widely reported to run meaningfully more expensive to build and operate than Taiwan-based ones (higher labor costs, permitting timelines, and immature local supply chain), which is a real, if secondary, margin risk as the US share of total capacity grows over the coming decade.

Risk Factors: Bull vs. Bear

Bull case

  • TSMC is effectively the sole capable supplier at the true leading edge and in advanced packaging (CoWoS) — a structural moat that is, if anything, widening as AI compute demand accelerates.
  • Margin expansion (high-60s gross margin vs. a historical ~50s range) reflects genuine pricing power, not a temporary cyclical peak.
  • US policy alignment (the Taiwan trade deal, CHIPS-style incentives) reduces geopolitical tail risk and comes with real financial upside (tax credits, tariff relief).
  • 2nm capacity and CoWoS packaging are reportedly sold out for years, giving unusual revenue visibility for a cyclical industry.

Bear case

Primary tail risk
A Taiwan Strait conflict or severe escalation is a low-probability, catastrophic-severity risk that cannot be meaningfully hedged through ordinary financial analysis or diversification within the sector — it would likely affect every semiconductor-exposed holding simultaneously.
  • Customer concentration is now arguably worse, not better: Nvidia alone plus its next two largest AI customers represent the vast majority of CoWoS capacity, tying results tightly to the continuation of the AI capex cycle.
  • Rising US-based capacity, at structurally lower margins than Taiwan, could dilute blended profitability as the mix shifts over the next decade.
  • Samsung and Intel are both racing to close the leading-edge gap with heavy state backing; a yield breakthrough at either would erode TSMC's pricing power.
  • Valuation already prices in a great deal of the AI growth story (see below) — multiple compression on any demand disappointment could be severe.
  • The export-control regime is a live policy variable, not a settled one; it could tighten again under a different administration or a new geopolitical flashpoint.

Valuation & Street View

The wide gap between trailing and forward P/E reflects a market pricing in substantial further earnings growth as the AI buildout continues. EV/EBITDA, a metric less distorted by TSMC's heavy depreciation load than P/E, sits around 18.5x per third-party estimates — a premium to the broader semiconductor sector consistent with TSMC's unique leading-edge position, but not an obviously extreme one given its growth and margin profile.
